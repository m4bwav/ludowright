# Worldbuilding and lore

Answers: how to grow a setting's history and lore without contradictions or a pile of loose hooks. Load it for lore bibles, timelines, faction histories and quest or scene briefs. Written 2026-11-14 from Robbins, Microscope (2011); items 10 to 14 and the adapting block added 2026-11-20 from Robbins, Microscope Explorer (Explorer page numbers). Book rules, unverified.

## Principles

1. Bookends first. Before any lore, write the world's first and last state on the timeline, one line each with a tone (hopeful or grim) and why. Every later entry sits between them. [p. 12]
2. Keep an exceptions list, not an inventory: what is in though a reader would not expect it, and what is out though they would. Leave out what the genre already implies. [p. 13-14]
3. Write each entry at one zoom level: an era gives the sweep, an incident says what visibly happened, a scene plays one moment. Flag entries that mix levels. [p. 20-21]
4. Outcome first. An incident states its result. One without a result is a hook, not canon; mark it open. [p. 21]
5. Three checks before an entry becomes canon: does it contradict anything written, does it serve the current focus, does it use something on the out list? [p. 20]
6. Keep a ledger of incidents mentioned but never written, and place each new entry before or after them. [p. 68]
7. Mark deliberate gaps as open in the doc. Meaning only the author knows is not canon. [p. 69]
8. Brief a quest or scene as a specific question with a built-in dilemma: "will X do Y, even though Z?", not "what happens next?". [p. 66-67]
9. Treat a contradiction found in review as a possible story seed before deleting it. [p. 75]
10. Grow lore in rounds with a narrow focus (a person, place or incident) that every addition must touch; rotate who sets it. Widen the focus when the lore feels dense or linear, narrow it when it feels remote. After each round one author may mark one specific thing (not a concept) as a legacy to revisit off-focus. [p. 18, 65; Explorer p. 23-24]
11. Settle content vetoes at setup, by category, never against someone's finished entry. After setup no new subject matter: surprising events are fine, a new kind of content is a premise change. Reopen setup only for a shared oversight. [Explorer p. 14-17]
12. The setup list may fix concepts as well as things: a tone, a realism default with listed exceptions, or a change axis (technology, faith, borders) that every new era must say how it moved or held. [Explorer p. 16]
13. Test each entry: can every reader picture it with no blank parts, and is its outcome stated? Every light or dark label carries a one-line reason. [Explorer p. 18-19]
14. Stuck: pick an important person or thing and add its missing arc point (birth, victory, failure, end), or introduce something new by its downfall and work back. [Explorer p. 20-21]

## Adapting a co-made world

When lore made by several authors becomes one game's setting, log every move: focus, expand and create are safe; ignore, remove and revise are riskier. Announce what you ignore, since a player may bring it back through a character. Prefer remove to revise, which overwrites others' work and all built on it. Test: does the game feel set in the world we made? Set the story away from known outcomes, or use a known ending as backdrop; a plot about undoing what players already know drains its stakes. [Explorer p. 74-77]

Scope: these favour big open settings. A small game narrows scope on purpose; apply them to its lore only.
