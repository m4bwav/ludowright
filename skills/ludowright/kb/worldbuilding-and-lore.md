# Worldbuilding and lore

Answers: how to grow a setting's history and lore without contradictions or a pile of loose hooks. Load it for lore bibles, timelines, faction histories and quest or scene briefs. Written 2026-11-14 from Robbins, Microscope (2011); book rules, unverified.

## Principles

1. Bookends first. Before any lore, write the world's first and last state on the timeline, one line each with a tone (hopeful or grim) and why. Every later entry sits between them. [p. 12]
2. Keep an exceptions list, not an inventory: what is in though a reader would not expect it, and what is out though they would. Leave out what the genre already implies. [p. 13-14]
3. Write each entry at one zoom level: an era gives the sweep, an incident says what visibly happened, a scene plays one moment. Flag entries that mix levels. [p. 20-21]
4. Outcome first. An incident states its result. One without a result is a hook, not canon; mark it open. [p. 21]
5. Three checks before an entry becomes canon: does it contradict anything written, does it serve the current focus, does it use something on the out list? [p. 20]
6. Keep a ledger of incidents mentioned but never written, and place each new entry before or after them. [p. 68]
7. Mark deliberate gaps as open in the doc. Meaning only the author knows is not canon. [p. 69]
8. Brief a quest or scene as a specific question with a built-in dilemma: "will X do Y, even though Z?", not "what happens next?". [p. 66-67]
9. Treat a contradiction found in review as a possible story seed before deleting it. [p. 75]

Scope: these favour big open settings. A small game narrows scope on purpose; apply them to its lore only.
